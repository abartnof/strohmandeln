# 05-POLY fragment: Tarock terms and table calls in German, Hungarian, Czech, Slovene and Polish

Sub-strand of note 05. Scope: (a) Czech, Hungarian, Slovene and Polish equivalents of the Austrian Tarock vocabulary, with the earliest attestation found; (b) how bids, announcements and card names were said aloud, from period German rulebooks and from fiction in all these languages.

Method: everything was fetched on 14 October 2026. Sources are saved under `research/sources/05/poly/`: archive.org `_djvu.txt` OCR texts (`ia_*.txt`, with long-s normalised copies `*.norm.txt`), Moravian Library Kramerius OCR pages (`kramerius/*.txt`), Naše řeč archive pages (`nr_art*.html`), fran.si dictionary pages (`fran_*.html`), pagat.com pages (`pagat_*.html` and `.txt`), Wikipedia wikitext (`wp_*.txt`), and saved archive.org full-text-search results (`fts_snippets_saved.txt`, `fts_slovene_czech.txt`).
- `[OCR]` means the quote comes from uncorrected OCR. Obvious OCR damage is lightly normalised, and the long s (ſ) is always normalised to s.
- `[FTS]` means only a full-text-search snippet could be read (the item is restricted). It is quoted exactly as the snippet shows it.
- `[2ary]` marks a modern secondary source (Wikipedia, pagat.com, Dummett 1980), used for anything not found in a period text.
- Google Books returned HTTP 429 (rate limit) for the whole session, so no Google Books material is used.

## 0. Headline findings

1. **Vienna's 1756 Tarock manual opens with an "Ode auf das Taroc Spiel"**, which may be the oldest Tarock verse in German. It names the honours in the order a player's luck brings them: "So bald ich nur den Scar geleget, / Den Mongue in meiner Hand erblickt / Setzt der Pagat sich an die Seite, … Und kommt der lustge Scüs darzu, / So sitz ich in vergnügter Ruh", and it ends on "Mich reizet und ergötzt die Ehre / Wenn mein Pagat den letzten sticht" [POLY-S1, pp. A2–A3]. It also muses on the etymology of Scüs and the French "Excuse"/Italian "Scusa" [POLY-S1]. [REVIEW RD: the 1756 text only "muthmaßt" a link, allows that it may run the other way round, and adds that such game etymologies are worth about as much as it matters ("von den mehresten dergleichen Spiel-Etymologien eben so viel zu halten seye, als sonsten daran wenig gelegen ist"). "Gives a folk etymology" overstates it; 05-table-talk (05-S12) describes it correctly.] (The ode itself belongs to the 05 verse section; it is listed here because the vocabulary comes from it.)
2. **The 1820s Viennese stage already used Tarock words as names for people.** Ferdinand Rosenau's farce *Scüs, Mond und Pagat* (Vienna, 1821) has three suitors called **Herr Dreyer**, **Herr Solo** and **Herr Ultimo**, plus a hapless **Tarokerl**. Their talismans are the Scüs, Mond and Pagat cards. It contains the table line **"Unnütze Mühe, der Scüs sticht ja den Mond"** ("Wasted effort, the Scüs takes the Mond anyway") and **"Ich habe den Pagat Ultimo gemacht!"** [POLY-S4]. This means "Dreier", "Solo" and "Ultimo" were common enough as bids by 1821 to work as comic names. [REVIEW RD: all quoted Rosenau lines verified in `sources/05/poly/ia_scsmondundpaga00rose_djvu.txt` (Wien 1821, Anton von Hapkul). Context: "Unnüße Mühe, der Scüs sticht ja den Mond" is Dreyer gloating over rival Solo's Mond talisman in a conjuring scene, not a table moment; the "Pagat Ultimo gemacht" / "ich habe ja noch einen Tarok" exchange is the dénouement. The printed libretto exists, so note 05c ("No text found") was wrong on this point; RD corrected 05c.]
3. **The core Strohmandeln call is "Ich nehme auf"** ("I take it up"), the only positive bid. The opponent may answer "Kontra" (Löw) [POLY-S32, Dummett pp. 458–459]. The older dummy game (Haas, before 1853) speaks of the straw man **"schmieren"** ("smearing", i.e. dropping counters) kings and court cards onto a player's tricks [POLY-S5, p. 577].
4. **The four nationalities share almost all the vocabulary, but each has its own doubling ladder and slang.**
   - Austria: Kontra, Re, Sub, Mord.
   - Slovenia: kontra, rekontra, subkontra, mordkontra.
   - Czechoslovakia: **flek, reflek, super**.
   - Polish Silesia: kontra, re, sub, mord.

   Hungarians bid with **"passz", "tartom" ("I hold"), "három/kettő/egy/szóló"**. They announce **"tulétroá"** (from French *tous les trois*) and call a partner with **"Hívom a tizenkilencest"** ("I call the nineteen") [POLY-S28, S30, S27].
5. **Hungarian literature has the best period banter.** In Jókai's people's play *Világszép leányok* (in *Színművek* II, 1895), Csibor announces "Tizenkét tarokk, tuletroa, pagát ultimo." The reply is **"Hohó! Hiszen nálam van a pagát!"** ("Ho-ho! But *I've* got the pagát!"), followed by great laughter and Csibor's line **"No még ilyen sem történt rajtam — ezen a héten"** ("Well, that's never happened to me before — this week") [POLY-S10, pp. 265–266]. Mikszáth's club sketch "Skiz, XXI. és I." gives the full doubling cry: **"Kontra az ultimónak, kontra a játéknak, kontra mindennek!"** ("Kontra on the ultimo, kontra on the game, kontra on everything!"), which brings every **kibic** (kibitzer) in the room to the table [POLY-S11].
6. **Czech has period slang names for the honours and a word for the kibitzer.** A 1934 *Naše řeč* snippet gives **"pagát harfička, škíz rohatej a mond plná čuňa"** ("the pagát is 'the little harp', the škíz 'the horned one', the mond 'the full sow'") [POLY-S21, FTS]. [REVIEW RD: the saved FTS snippet reads "slove pagát harfička, škíz rohatej a mond plná -© čuňa"; the OCR debris "-©" sits between "plná" and "čuňa" and was dropped silently, so a word may be missing; "full sow" is a literal rendering of an unclear idiom, and the snippet gives neither region nor author. Treat the meaning of the third name as UNVERIFIED; "harfička" and "rohatej" (the horned one) read plainly.] A Kratonohy record from 1890 gives **"Čumák je ten, kdo čumí hráčům do karet a radí"** ("A čumák is someone who gawps into the players' cards and gives advice") [POLY-S19]. In Hanák dialect a slow player is teased with **"Senko, nehoď náhle! Rok je dlóhé!"** ("Don't rush it, the year is long!") and told **"Neplké a hré"** ("Stop yakking and play") [POLY-S15, p. 191].
7. **Etymologies:**
   - *Sküs/škis/skíz* comes from French *excuse* / Italian *scusa*. The 1756 manual only conjectures a link (see the review note in finding 1; it also considers the reverse direction), and Snoj suggests the card got the name from the player's habit of saying "pardon" when using it [POLY-S1; S26].
   - *Pagat* comes from Italian *bagatto/bagattino* ("small coin"). It reached Slovene "via Hungarian *pagát* or German *Pagat*" [POLY-S26].
   - *Trull* comes from *tous les trois*. The chain runs Ulmann *tous les trois* → Werner *Troule* → Unger *Trul* → *Trull*. *Königstrull* (from *tous les quatre*) is "in defiance of etymology" (Dummett) [POLY-S32, p. 453].
   - *Uhu* is first found in 1902 in the Hungarian game Alsós, and for Tarock II in Unger 1937 [POLY-S33, 2ary].
   - *Quapil* is a Czech surname, "kvapil" = "he hurried" [POLY-S33, 2ary].
   - *Rostopschin* is a bonus for two consecutive tricks with high tarocks, announced as you play: "Ross!" … "Topschin!". The only report is Torberg, *Tante Jolesch* (1975), p. 217, as quoted on Wikipedia [POLY-S33/S34, 2ary].
8. **Two-hand Tarock is older and wider than the Viennese sources alone show.** A Polish card almanac printed in Breslau in 1821 describes **"Tarok en deux"**: the dealer gives 25 cards to the opponent and 28 to himself, and the "remaining 25 cards are laid aside and not counted" [POLY-S8]. This is a hunch for the rules strand. [REVIEW RD: the Polish text is a translation of the same Cäsar/Mößler "Taroc en deux" text found in Berlin 1800, Vienna 1805 and Berlin 1810 (note 03 S1-S3; the Polish wording follows it sentence by sentence: "Rozdający daje po 5. Kart, przeciwnikowi swemu 25. sobie zaś 28. Kart; reszta 25. Kart kładzie się na bok, i nie rachuje się do gry"). So it is a further witness to one text, not independent evidence that two-hand Tarock was "wider". The 1821 date is confirmed by the archive.org record (date 1821, Korn, Wrocław) although the OCR title page is garbled.]

### Hunch-level etymological notes
- **Scüs as Pickelhering/Harlekin**: German sources (1797 "Pickelhering", 1853 "Harlekin") describe the card's picture as a stage clown. Snoj's "apology" etymology [S26] fits the 1756 phrase "sich nicht an die Regeln des Spiels binden darf". That makes the card a privileged fool, which supports a comic "pardon me" line when the Sküs is played.
- **"Mongur"** (1853, quoting the Vienna Haas text) looks like a Viennese spoken form of French *monde* / "Mongue". It is worth using as a period pronunciation of the Mond.
- **Slovene *škis* also means "an unpleasant person"** (SSKJ²: "slabš. neprijeten, zoprn človek … kot psovka tu imaš, ti škis") [S25]. Do not use it as an insult (kindness rule).
- **Chod Czech *pagátl*** was a 20th-century insult for an upstart townsman ("Šosáci, měšťáci byli titulováni … pišišvor a pagátl") [S23, Naše řeč 1976]. It is a class insult, so it is recorded here and must not be used.

## 2. How calls were said: formulae by language

### German (Austria), period → modern
- **Naming a card aloud (1867)**: "„Tarok fünf," sagte Janos. „Caro König," flüsterte Peter. „Treff Aß," bemerkte Imre. „Tarok achtzehn hat Achmet," las Peter." Then, turning his card: "„Mond!"" [S6, *Banditen im Franck* (1867)]. The context is grim: a draw to decide who must die. Only the naming pattern is borrowed, not the scene.
- **Announcing taroks "with/without"**: "setzt man … beim Ansagen von Taroks hinzu: avec (mit), d. h. der Pagat ist dabei, oder nicht, — sans (ohne)" [S5, 1853]. In 1801: "Wer Tarocs meldet, ist gehalten zu sagen, ob sie mit oder ohne den Pagat sind, in welchem letzten Falle auf den Pagat gefordert wird" [S3].
- **Excusing**: "ich skisire mich" [S5].
- **Honneurs announced in a story (1810)**: "man sagte die Honneurs an. Drey Damen mit Skis Treff-kavallerie mit Skis, sagte der König" [S37, FTS snippet, W. Sternberg, *Sammlung interessanter Anekdoten* (1810)].
- **Bidding (Tapp-Tarock, before 1853)**: Vorhand "zeigt … einen Tapper an. Nun kann die Mittelhand Dreier ansagen"; "Einer aus der Hinterhand declarirte einen Solo"; "wenn nicht rund herum gepaßt wird" [S5, pp. 560–562].
- **Bidding (later sources, via Dummett)**: "a player bidding a Dreier says ‘Ich spiele' (Löw) … ‘Ich schaue hinein' (Unger); to keep priority, ‘Halte ich' (Beck, Babsch) or ‘Mein Spiel' (Löw); ‘Ich tappe' to pass … is replaced in later works by ‘Weiter'"; and "it is now customary to announce only ‘Pagat' instead of ‘Pagat ultimo'" [S32, pp. 453–454, 2ary].
- **Strohmandeln (c.1890 onward)**: the bid is "‘Ich nehme auf'"; the dealer may reply "‘Kontra'" (Löw); Trull or four Kings are declared during play "just before he plays one of them" [S32, pp. 458–459, 2ary].
- **Modern Königrufen**: "Mein Spiel", contracts Rufer, Dreier, Solo, Piccolo, Bettel …; "kontra the game", "kontra the king ultimo" [S31, 2ary].
- **Rostopschin (Café Central, interwar; Torberg 1975)**: "bei der ersten Karte mit „Ross!" und bei der zweiten mit „Topschin!", sonst galt's nicht" [S33, quoting Torberg p. 217; 2ary, not seen in the original].

### Hungarian
- **Bidding**: "Passz – Három – Kettő – Passz – Tartom – Passz"; "tartom" = "I hold it" [S28, 2ary].
- **Announcements**: "nyolc tarokk, hívom a tizenkilencest, tulétroá, négykirály, passz" [S28, 2ary].
- **Period announcement strings**:
  - "Tuletroa, pagat ultimo." (Gaal, *A peleskei nótárius*, text of the 1881 edition; first staged c.1838 [UNVERIFIED]) [S9]
  - "Tizenkét tarokk tuletroa pagát ultimo." / "Rekontra!" (Jókai 1895) [S10]
  - "«Solo, tous les trois, pagát ultimo!»" / "«Kontra a tous les trois-nak!»" / "«Kontra az ultimónak, kontra a játéknak, kontra mindennek!»" / "«rekontra»" (Mikszáth, c.1885) [S11]

### Czech

- **Hanák dialect (Karel Křen, d. 1907; published 1924)**: "starosta hlásil deset taroků a pakáta" (the mayor announced ten taroks and the pagát); a run of trumps called out as it is played: "Tade to máte: osnást, devatenást, dvacet, škýz! Ešče máte stech na mundo a ste po ftákách!" ("There you go: eighteen, nineteen, twenty, škýz! … and you're done for") [S15, p. 191] [OCR]. "Po ftákách" literally means "after the birds". It is probably an idiom for "finished", and the reading is unverified.

### Slovene
- **Modern**: kontra / rekontra / subkontra / mordkontra; contracts *tri, dve, ena, solo tri, solo dve, solo ena, berač, solo brez, odprti berač, barvni valat brez, valat brez*, and *klop* [S27, 2ary]. ## 6. Hunches followed

- **"Scüs, Mond und Pagat" as a book title (archive.org FTS).** Yielded Rosenau's 1821 Viennese farce, in which the bids themselves are characters. A strong find. [REVIEW RD: confirmed.]
- **Hungarian literary tarokk (FTS "tuletroá", "skiz pagát").** Yielded Jókai, Mikszáth and Gaal: rich banter.
- **Czech kibitzer word.** While looking for card slang in *Naše řeč*, found *čumák* (1890) and *Mám bezpečila* (a card player's cry on getting an ace; 1934 [S19-adjacent, art. 2806], not Tarock).
- **Rostopschin.** Not a period rulebook term that I could find. It is a liqueur in a Viennese price list (FTS, b21504696) and Count Rostopchin in many texts. Its only Tarock use is Torberg's anecdote as quoted on Wikipedia. Hunch: Café Central players named the XVII–XVIII double after the Russian count/liqueur because of the "Ross" (horse) pun. [UNVERIFIED]
- **"Pagat ultimo" outside cards.** Count Moriz Esterházy's racehorse *Pagat ultimo* raced in 1898 (FTS: Polish/Austrian racing papers), and *Die Fackel* 1916 used "Pagat-Ultimo" as a headline metaphor (FTS). This shows the phrase was everyday Austro-Hungarian idiom. [FTS only]
- **Mondfang in German.** archive.org FTS gives only "Sonn- und Mondfang" (a Swabian farce). No period Tarock use was found.
- **Slovene period table talk (dLib keyword search, Pleteršnik).** Nothing before 1990 except the bare dictionary entry.

## 7. Gaps / leads not followed

- Ulmann, *Illustrirtes Wiener Tarokbuch* (1888) and *Das Buch der Familienspiele* (1890), Werner (1883) and Unger (1937) were not found in full text (Google Books blocked). They are the key sources for the late-19th-century German calls.
- Vanderheid's 1886 "Regeln für Kibitze" (Vienna, Wenedikt) would be ideal for kibitzer talk. Only Horr's title entry was seen.
- The Munich 1881 "Deutschtarokspielen" glossary of "Provincialismen und vulgäre Bezeichnungen" (Horr no. 359). Not located.
- Arcanum (Hungarian press), the full Kramerius run before 1900 (*škýz* hits from the 1800s were religious texts, i.e. false positives), and the Slovene dLib full text were not searched in depth.
- Gaal's premiere date and Jókai's play date were not verified.
- The 1934 *Naše řeč* "harfička / rohatej / plná čuňa" article was seen only as a snippet.
