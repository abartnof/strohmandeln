# 11. Strohmandeln and two-hand Tarock in the Habsburg Empire's other languages

Fetched 14 October 2026. Saved sources: `research/sources/11/` (`cz/` Kramerius OCR and highlight snippets; `hu/` Parlaghy 1888 PDF and text, Pallas pages; `anno_foreign.jsonl` and `anno_agramer1895.txt` ANNO snippet runs; `ia/fts_run.txt` archive.org full-text search). Tag `[OCR]` = OCR text I read and normalised; `[snip]` = only a search-index highlight could be read (the page text is restricted or the source returns snippets only); `[2ary]` = secondary. Translations are mine.

## Headline findings

1. **Czech has a borrowed name, "štromandl", with its own Czech grammar.** The Czech genitive/accusative "štromandla" appears in 1891, 1892, 1917 and 1930 (and the form "stromandle" in 1912) in the Czech press [REVIEW RE: the 1912 form is "stromandle" (OCR), not "štromandla"; the 1930 snippet shows no card context at all (no card word on the page, checked via Kramerius search), so 1930 is **not** verified as the game and could be a surname (a Czech surname Štromandl exists: Naše pravda 27 Oct 1953, "soudruhem Štromandlem", a tractor driver); 1917 is supported by "karty", "hráli" and "sázku" on the same page], and a Czech etiquette manual of 1932 has a section headed "Štromandl. Taroky ve dvou" ("Tarock for two") [11-S1, S2, S3, S4, S5]. It is clearly a loanword adapted from the Viennese "Strohmandl" (the *-h-* dropped, *š-* added, declined like a masculine noun).
2. **A Czech clerical joke (1891, repeated 1892): a parish priest tests a would-be assistant teacher by asking "Can you play štromandl?"** The answer "I can" got him the post. It was told at a teachers' assembly (Moravian Czech assistant teachers; the 1891 speaker says appointments rest on "malichernostech a hloupostech", trivia and follies) [11-S2, S3]. [REVIEW RE: the text never says "cards" or "Tarock" next to štromandl; that it is a card game is inferred from the neighbouring questions "Hrajete v karty? Co hráváte nejraději?" in the same speech. "Classic parish-parlour game" is the note's inference, not a statement of the sources.] It is usable as a tutor/opening line.
3. **The Hungarian name "Strohmandli" could not be confirmed in any Hungarian-language PERIOD source I could reach.** [REVIEW RE: a modern Hungarian-language page does use it: scordatura.hu/stuszivadasz/paros.html, "A 'strohmandli' egy különleges tarokk ... két játékos játszhatja ... A pakli 42 lapos", which says its description differs slightly from "a Jánoska Antal-féle tarokkismertető" (a Hungarian tarokk guide; a lead). Mayr & Sedlaczek (Wiener Zeitung 2007, sources/01/cites/wz.txt) also say "Strohmandeln wird heute ... auch in Ungarn gespielt. Dort heißt es Strohmandli", and Stuf7023 (sources/08/pdf) lists "Strohmandli (Ungarn, 2 Spieler ...)". So "Dummett-only" below is wrong; none of these is period evidence.] Parlaghy Kálmán's *Kártyajátékok könyve* (Budapest, 1888), which does describe Hungarian Tarokk at length, says that of the many Tarock games only two are "rendesen" (ordinarily, usually) played in Hungary, four-hand "Paskievics" and three-hand tarokk, and describes neither Strohmandl nor any two-hand form [11-S6]. So Dummett's "Strohmandli" (p. 457 ff.: "or, in Hungary, Strohmandli", confirmed in sources/06/other/dummett/p4.txt) remains second-hand for the period. It may reflect a later (after 1888) or Austrian-influenced use; nothing here contradicts it.
4. **In Hungary the German loan "strohmann" was used for the whist dummy by 1888** ("négynek kell játszani, vagy helyettesül egy vagy legfölebb két strohmannal", "it must be played by four, or one or at most two Strohmänner in their place") [11-S6, printed p. 124, section *Whist*; fixed by REVIEW RE from the page images/running heads; the note's "p. 99-ish" was wrong]. So the word crossed the border for whist, but not (in that book) for Tarock.
5. **A "first European Tarock Congress" at Tepl (Bohemia), reported 2 Feb 1893, fixed unified rules for "Strohmandl", "Tapper" and "Königsrufer"**, with 300 attendees "partly players, partly kibitzers", and a closing "Tapper" with horn signals for every successful Valat and trumpet blasts for each announced Pagat ultimo [11-S7]. (Rules not recorded in the report; a lead.) [REVIEW RE: the Prager Tagblatt 2 Feb 1893 p. 6 copy (ANNO_ptb18930202) says "Aus Tepl wird dem 'Fremdbl.' geschrieben", i.e. it was taken from the Vienna Fremden-Blatt; the Brünn paper's Tepl item is in the general news before the "Brünn, 2. Februar" section, issue date 2.2.1893 confirmed by Kramerius. The report reads partly like a jocular item (fanfares for every Valat and Pagat ultimo); treat "unified rules" as unverified.]
6. **There was a Bohemian social club named "Strohmandl"** ("Geselligkeitsverein 'Strohmandl'", Bohemia, 20 May 1906, in a comic sketch) [11-S8]: the game's name was a byword for convivial parlour company.
7. **Slovene, Polish, Italian, Croatian: no trace of the name or of a two-hand description in ANNO/Kramerius.** [REVIEW RE: but note 05d item 8 reports a Polish almanac printed in Breslau in 1821 describing "Tarok en deux"; this headline must not be read as "no Polish two-hand description exists".] Searches of ANNO and the Czech libraries for Slovene/Polish/Italian/Croatian/Hungarian phrases ("tarok za dva", "tarok u dvoje", "taroki we dwóch", "tarocco a due", "tarocchi in due", "kétszemélyes tarokk" and variants) returned 0 phrase hits; the Slovene forms "štromandl/strohmandla" also returned 0 in ANNO (section 6). Absence in a newspaper index is weak evidence, but it suggests the game stayed a German-Austrian/Czech-lands phenomenon.
8. **Hungarian Tarokk's table vocabulary, as printed in 1888, adds colourful phrases** (section 4): "tarok fekszik" (a tarock lies in the discard), "segít a XX-as" (the XX helps = calling a partner by card), "sír és nevet" (the fifth player, the dealer, "weeps and laughs" with the bidder's opponents), "ugratás" ("goading, egging on" [REVIEW RE: not "jumping"]; a four-hand variant in which one misleads the others by a smaller bid or false invitation), and the contra ladder with "Hirsch" and "Super contra" [11-S6].

## 1. Czech

### 1.1 Štromandl / štromandla in Czech print
- **Společenský rádce pro všechny životní okolnosti** (*Social adviser for all of life's occasions*), 1932. Contents list (printed on p. 495): "Štromandl .... 370 / Taroky ve třech .... 371" [REVIEW RE: the note and note 06 said "p. 369"; the Kramerius highlight of the contents page 495 gives the entry "Štromandl ... 370", 369 being the preceding entry]; p. 370 heading: ". Štromandl. Taroky ve dvou" [11-S1, snip; the same p. 370 snippet shows the heading follows rule 15 of another game ("Hrají-li čtyři hráči, snímají se karty vždy do kříže")]. Book: Gottfried Andreas (Čajčík, Karel, adaptor), Moravská Ostrava-Přívoz: I. Buchsbaum, 1932 (BIBLIO_MODS). Kramerius returns the page text as restricted (403 on `TEXT_OCR`), so I could read only the index highlight: the heading, not the rules. This is the first source found that has a Czech section on the game (only the heading was seen, not rules); the book is the same one strand 06 reported as 403. Rules still unread: try a library reading room or the NDK reading-room service. I did not probe the restricted page word by word via the search index.
- **Česká škola** 20 Jan 1891, p. 63 [OCR, 11-S2]. A speaker at a teachers' assembly lists the "petty and foolish" criteria on which posts are given: "Tak na př. představil jsem se jednou, žádaje o poduč. místo, p. faráři. Ten se mne tázal, umím-li hráti štromandla. 'Umím,' odvětil jsem. 'Tak to jisté dostanete!' ujištoval mne a — dostal jsem. (Pohnutí. Výborně.)" = "So for example I once introduced myself to the parish priest, applying for an assistant-teacher post. He asked me whether I could play štromandl. 'I can,' I answered. 'Then you will certainly get it!' he assured me, and I got it. (Stir. Hear, hear.)"
- **Vychovatel** 5 March 1892, p. 108 [OCR, 11-S3]. A Catholic school paper repeats the anecdote critically, quoting the teacher: asked by a priest when applying "umím-li hráti štromandla? Umím, řekl jsem; pak to dostanete, ujišťoval — a dostal jsem", and complaining that the speaker was belittling church and priest. This shows the story was widely told, and that "hráti štromandla" (to play štromandl) is the idiom.
- **Lidové noviny** 11 Feb 1912, p. 1 [OCR, 11-S9]: a café sketch about a very old regular, "pan Joachim Jellinek — ... vždy čilý a bystrozraký a ač je mu osmdesát roků, sleduje stále ještě všecky karetní hry od mariáše až po 'štromandle' s neochabující svěžestí duševní" ("always lively and sharp-eyed, and although he is eighty, he still follows all card games from mariáš up to štromandl with undiminished mental freshness" [REVIEW RE: OCR reads "stromandle"; "sleduje" = follows/watches; translation faithful]). The OCR column is jumbled and the venue is not clear. Štromandl is placed at the end of a list of card games ("od ... až po"); that it is the "most thought-demanding" is the note's inference only (contrast Pester Lloyd 16 Oct 1880, where Strohmandl is "den Geist nicht besonders anstrengend").
- **Právo lidu** 3 Mar 1917, p. 6: "...štromandla o sázku 100 koř[un]" ("... of štromandl for a stake of 100 crowns") [11-S10, snip; page text restricted].
- **Lidové noviny** 12 Jul 1930, p. 1: "...štromandla. Plno je však ..." [11-S10, snip; restricted].
- **Mährische heimatkundliche Mitteilungen** 1966, p. 228: "[strohmandla], ať toho jednou na[...]" [11-S10, snip, 20th-c., not period].
- A modern children's book, *Pohádka o Lvíčkovi* (2010, p. 10), glosses "štróhmandla — to jsou taroky ve dvou" ("štróhmandl — that is Tarock for two") [11-S10, snip; modern, not period, but shows the word still circulating as a gloss].
- Spelling variants seen in the OCR index: štromandl, stromandl(e), štróhmandl(a), strohmandla. Each is a Czech-adapted form of Viennese Strohmandl. The "Štrohmandl" hits are otherwise a modern surname (Jan Strohmandl, Defence University, Brno).
- Strand 06's note that "Štrohmandl" in Kramerius found only a surname is right for that spelling; the productive spellings are **štromandl-** and **strohmandla** (declined). Strand 06's "štramandl" (modern wiki) had 0 hits in the Kramerius index.

The book itself is a L'Hombre treatise; the Tarock list is an aside.
- A snippet of Prager Tagblatt 24 Sep 1920 gives a rule fragment: "Beim 'Strohmandl' muß der Aufnehmer, um zu gewinnen, mindestens zwei leere Blätter über 36 haben" (the OCR reads "86") [snip, from `research/sources/anno/strohmandl.jsonl`]: at Strohmandl the declarer ("Aufnehmer") must hold at least two empty cards over 36 to win. ### 1.3 Other Czech threads
- **"Taroky ve dvou" as an ordinary phrase.** Kramerius shows "odpoledne v taroky — ve dvou!" in *Rozkvět* (1926, p. 2) and "malý tarok ve dvou?" in the novel *Loučení* (1959/1965) [11-S13, snip]. The 1926 line is a plain remark about afternoon Tarock for two; it shows the Czech descriptive phrase alongside the loan "štromandl". The 1950s lines are modern.
- **"Taroky s honéry"** shouted from the back of a ball crowd in a 1911 humorous story: "'Taroky s honéry —!' bylo slyšeti kdesi vzadu hrubý hlas" ("Tarock with honneurs!" a coarse voice was heard somewhere at the back) [OCR, 11-S14, *Dennice novověku*, 26 Jan 1911, p. 7]. The cry interrupts a man telling how he declared his love to his mother-in-law, in a pause of the tale; that it is a stock "catch-phrase" is the note's inference, only this one occurrence was seen. It shows "taroky s honéry" as a Czech catch-phrase for the game with honour announcements (cf. Czech *honéry* for the honours); usable as a gag shout (the player who "cries out the honours at the wrong moment").
- **"hastroš"**: strand 06 noted the 1844 *Oliver Twist* footnote equating "s hastrošem hráti" with English "dummy". In Kramerius, *hastroš* is widespread (6,870 hits, mostly a stock character, the "Hastrman/Hastroš" of folk tale, and a theatre figure) and is used in 1895 *Výbor her a zábav mládeže* in a children's game "hráči dají se hastrošem..."; I found **no Czech Tarock use** of hastroš (one 1911 hit pairs "hastroš" and "taroky" only in the same issue page, not the same sentence) [11-S14]. Nothing for the Tarock dummy.
- *České taroky* (Brousková, 1994) and the Khestl and Omasta/Ravik card-game books are modern; they are the likely places for Czech rules of "taroky ve dvou" but are post-1990 and not period [Kramerius listing; not opened].

## 2. Hungarian

### 2.1 What Parlaghy 1888 says
Source: Parlaghy Kálmán, *Kártyajátékok könyve: a legelterjedtebb hazai és külföldi kártyajátékok leírása, a hamis játék, a roulette, hazárdjátékok s játékbarlangok leírásával ... és kártyaműszavak szótárával bővítve*, Budapest: Lampel, 1888, 210 pp. PDF from REAL-EOD (MTA Library) <https://real-eod.mtak.hu/6697/>; saved with my `pdftotext` OCR [OCR; 11-S6]. (The REAL-EOD catalogue lists a second copy under the name Porzsolt Kálmán, a likely catalogue confusion; I used the Parlaghy record.)
- Section "Társas játékok tarok kártyával. Tarok", printed pp. 126-141 for the four-hand game, pp. 139 ff. for the three-hand game [REVIEW RE: pages fixed from the page images; PDF page = printed page + 2; the book's title page carries no year, 1888 comes from the REAL-EOD catalogue, while the printer's sheet footers read "62-1889. Kártyakönyv", so 1888/1889 is not certain]. It says: "Nálunk Magyarországon az oly sok tarok játékokból, rendesen csak kétfélét játszanak a Négyes vagy máskép Paskievics és a hármas tarokkot." = "Here in Hungary, of the many Tarock games, only two kinds are usually played: the four-hand one, otherwise called Paskievics, and three-hand tarokk." It adds that the author could find no guiding work "even in a foreign language" and that "a hány ház annyi szokás" (as many houses, as many customs) applies, though the essentials are played uniformly across the country [11-S6].
- The pack: "A teljes tarokk kártya 78 lapból áll ..., az általunk leirni szándékolt kétféle játékhoz azonban csak 42 lapot vesznek": the full 78-card pack, but **only 42 cards** are used for the games he describes (22 trumps, K-Q-C-J of each suit, and the ace of hearts and diamonds, the ten of spades and clubs). He names the trumps' "honneurs" as the Skiz, XXI and I ("Pagat") [11-S6].
- **No two-hand game appears**: no "Strohmandl", no "kétszemélyes". Searching the whole OCR text for *strohm*, *mandl*, *szalma*, *ketten*, *kétszemélyes* gives only the whist reference (and other, non-Tarock uses of "ketten") [11-S6; REVIEW RE: confirmed; the book's table of contents also lists no two-hand Tarokk]. Hence Hungary's printed 1888 repertoire does not include Strohmandeln, which does not contradict Dummett's "or, in Hungary, Strohmandli"; that it was "only a later or border usage" is the note's speculation, not Dummett's; Parlaghy neither confirms nor refutes.
- Hungarian "Paskievics" is the 4-hand game named after Field Marshal Paskevich (Russian commander in the 1849 war [REVIEW RE: this identification is not in either source; Pester Lloyd says only "dem genannten großen Feldherrn"]): Pester Lloyd 27 Sep 1875 [OCR, 11-S15] says of a table of four: "wie man weiß, ist dieses Land dem genannten großen Feldherrn zu hohem Danke verpflichtet, weshalb man auch das beliebte Tarokspiel nach ihm benannt hat" ("as is well known this country owes the Field Marshal great thanks, which is why the beloved Tarock game was named after him"), and "wenn Jemand 'Paskievics' spielt (vorausgesetzt, der Jemand ist gewissenhaft ...), so ist die Außenwelt todt für ihn und es ist leichter, einen betenden Türken als einen Paskievics-Spieler zu einer Antwort zu bewegen" ("when someone plays Paskievics, the outside world is dead to him, and it is easier to make a praying Turk than a Paskievics player answer"). The irony (the 1849 Russian intervention is "thanked") is political; the second line is a gentle gag about concentration usable as banter ("the outside world is dead to him"). Leave out the Turk comparison.
- Agramer Zeitung 26 Feb 1895 [snip, 11-S16], an article on the history of Tarock: "...rufen gespielt, für zwei Spieler entstand das combinationsreiche Strohmann-Tarok. Mit 42 Karten wird das Ungarische Tarok, das amüsante Paskie[vics] ..." = "...[-]rufen played; for two players arose the combination-rich Strohmann-Tarock. With 42 cards the Hungarian Tarok is played, the amusing Paskie[vics]...". It positions Strohmann-Tarok as the two-player game, and the 42-card Hungarian game as separate. [REVIEW RE: the claim that the same article appears in the Klagenfurter Zeitung 1888 and the Brünn paper of 1887 is unverified: the snippets have different wording; only "Strohmann-Tarok (en deux)" recurs.] Klagenfurter Zeitung 30 Mar 1888 p. 1 and the Brünn paper of 22 Oct 1887 (strand 06) have: "Das 'Strohmann-Tarok' (en deux) läßt vermöge seiner ganz eigenartigen Zusammenstellung eine Fülle von Spielmöglichkeiten ..." [snip, 11-S16].

### 2.2 Hungarian table vocabulary in Parlaghy 1888 [OCR, 11-S6]
Beyond strand 05d's literary lines, Parlaghy records how the game was actually run. Items that could become banter (with the line's game moment; all fine under the kindness rule):
- **"Tarok fekszik"** ("a tarock is lying"). When a player has put a trump into the discard (skart) against a bidder, he must say "tarok fekszik" (or "két/három tarok fekszik") at once, per the rules (printed p. 128) [REVIEW RE: the duty is on those who laid the discard ("skart") against the bidder, and the call is made to the bidder; "skart" = discard, not talon]. Game moment: a discard that contains trumps. Line: "Tarok fekszik!" / "A trump lies in the discard!"
- **"Segít a XX-as"** / "hív a XIII-as tarok": the bidder names the trump that calls his partner ("tegyük fel: segít a XX-ás"), with "invit" (invitation) possible for the "kettes" bid but not for "hármas" (printed pp. 128 and 131) [REVIEW RE: "pair game" was a misreading; kettes/egyes/solo are bids named after the number of talon cards taken]. Game moment: calling the partner by card. (This is a four-hand partnership game; the claim that in two-hand play the idea survives as the dummy's help is the note's speculation, not in Parlaghy.)
- **"Hezitálás"** for the bidding contest. Game moment: bidding.
- **Contra ladder**: contra, recontra, rerecontra; "Hirsch = a contrázás egy foka a tarokban", "Super contra" in the glossary (glossary pp. III-VI; the ladder in the text is on printed p. 131: contra, recontra, rerecontra, supercontra, then "Hirsch" and "Pápa", "but these are very rare") [11-S6]. Game moment: doubling.
- **"Sír és nevet"** ("weeps and laughs"): with five players the dealer plays "sírés nevet", against the bidder, sharing every win and loss; known to everyone as playing "Biká-val" ("with the bull"). It makes the game more hazardous. Game moment: a bid taken by a rival with the dealer in on the other side (printed p. 138; the dealer pays the bidder what the bidder wins and receives what he loses, i.e. is in effect on the opponents' side).
- **"Ugratás"** ("jumping"): a variant where the whole aim is to lead the opponents astray by a smaller bid or false invitation (printed pp. 138-139; "ugrat" = to goad/egg on). Tutor note for bluff-bidding.
- **Tariff**: "Lehet mondani egész Magyarországon egyöntetű a négyes tarok díjszabása": the four-hand ("négyes") tariff is uniform across Hungary [REVIEW RE: the note said "three-hand scale": "hármas" here is the bid "three" = 1 point]: three-game 1, two-game 2, one-game 3, solo 4 (some 1-2-4-8), tous les trois and four kings 2-2 announced / 1-1 unannounced, **pagat ultimo 10 announced / 5 unannounced**, volat 6x announced / 3x unannounced, **XXI-fogás 21 unannounced / 42 announced**, plus 1 and 2 for eight and nine trumps in hand (printed pp. 137-138) [11-S6]. These are Hungarian point values, not Austrian Strohmandeln values, but they are the closest period figures for the shared bonus family in the Empire's other half.
- **"Kettes / egyes / solo"** (two / one / solo) are Hungarian names of bid levels (by number of talon cards taken: hármas 3, kettes 2, egyes 1, solo 0).
- Hungarian whist took the German word: "strohmannal" (printed p. 124).
- Glossary (pdf pp. 203-206, printed III-VI; the entry gives "Paskievicz = népes tarok 127. l."): lists *Paskievicz = népes tarok* ("populous Tarock") and the trump numbers by Hungarian names (*Huszonegyes tarok*, *Tizenkilenczes tarok*...) and *Skíz = a tarok kártya legerősebb ...* [11-S6].

## 5. Observations and hunches

- **The Czech lands are where Strohmandl lived most visibly outside Vienna**: the card-table anecdote of the parish priest (1891/92), the Tepl congress (1893), the club called "Strohmandl" (1906), "štromandl" as a Czech noun (1892-1932), the 1887 Brno article on "Strohmann-Tarock (en deux)" as the "favourite game of all thinking Tarock friends" (strand 06). A fully "period" Moravian/Bohemian flavour (priest's parlour, village inn, train compartment, Prague café) is thus better grounded than a Hungarian one.
- **Hungarian Tarokk and Strohmandl look like separate traditions**: Hungary's 1888 repertoire is the 42-card 3/4-hand game with "pagat ultimo" and "XXI-fogás"; Agramer Zeitung 1895 explicitly separates "Ungarische Tarok (42 cards)" from "Strohmann-Tarok" for two. A game designer could keep the Hungarian vocabulary as colour for the bonuses without putting it on the Strohmandl rules. (Hunch.)
- **The word itself travelled as a loan with local endings** (Czech štromandla, Hungarian strohmann for whist, probably Hungarian "Strohmandli" with a *-li*). Hunch: the Hungarian "-li" is a German-Hungarian (Pest/Pressburg) rendering of Viennese "-l", and Dummett's form may come from a Hungarian German-language source rather than from Hungarian-language text. Unverified.
- **Strohmandl as a pun**: the "Strohmann zur Strohmandl-Partie" gag (1893) shows how naturally the game's name invited jokes about stand-in persons; a banter pool could use "Strohmann" jokes ("Who's sitting there, me or my straw man?") for the dummy piles.
