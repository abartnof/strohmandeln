## Headline findings

1. **The name "Strohmandeln" is attested at least five years earlier than the Wikipedia article's 1829.** A Vienna/Prague
   book of 1824 (G. Haas) lists it among the kinds of Tarok: "Quadrille, en trois, Pagatjagen, Strohmandeln, Tappen zu drey und
   vier Personen" [06-S3]. Dummett (1980) confirms it is the cursory two-player adaptation of Tarok-Tappen found in the Austrian
   books from the 1829 Neuestes allgemeines Spielbuch on, but not in the 1821 Anweisung or the 1830 German Neuestes Spiel-Taschenbuch [06-S1].
2. **Two quite different games bear the name.** The *early form* (first half of 19th c.): three hands of 16 cards, the middle
   hand a face-down dummy ("Strohmann") from which the trick-winner takes the top card into his tricks. The *later form* (1880s at
   the latest, perhaps much earlier): 15 cards in hand plus three face-down 4-card piles ("Straw men") per player, whose exposed top
   card is pulled into the hand when it is a King or a Tarock [06-S1; 06-S2]. Dummett calls the early one "rather jejune" [06-S1].
3. **The later form has a primary source (undated; Dummett guesses "the first decade of this century", the 1887 and 1894 press show the name was current earlier) that I could read in full: K. Werner, *Das moderne Tarokspiel*, 2nd ed.
   (Hartleben, Vienna, undated), pp. 129-135.** It calls it "Das Strohmann-Tarok (in der Umgangssprache Strohmanndl genannt)" and states
   the signature mechanic exactly [06-S2]. The Werner preface says Strohmann-Tarok was added in this edition as "ältere, interessante
   Spielvarianten von zwar geringerer Verbreitung" [06-S2b] - i.e. already regarded as an older game of lesser circulation.
4. **What is unique to Strohmandeln (later form):** the *automatic pull* of Kings and Tarocks from the exposed top of each pile into the
   hand, and the way the pile is therefore a "filter" that leaves only plain suit cards (non-Kings) as exposed blockers [06-S1; 06-S2]. No
   other relative I found has a pull-in rule; in all the others (Officers' Skat, Officers' Schafkopf, Sjavs, French Tarot for two, the Italian
   Baronetti) the exposed card just sits there and is playable until played, and is replaced only by being played [06-S5; 06-S6; 06-S1].
5. **Strong hunch with textual support - the root is a dead hand ("mort") in two-hand Tarot, 1781/1800.** De Gébelin (1781) describes two-handed
   French Tarot played with a *mort*, "a dead hand of twenty-five cards"; German books from the Neuester Spielalmanach (Berlin, 1800) on give
   two-hand Tarok with a dead hand "not looked at until the end of the round"; Dummett thinks it goes back to about 1750 [06-S1]. The "Taroc en deux"
   of 1800 (Berlin NSa per Dummett; the Vienna Mößler edition read in note 01 is 1805, and there the 25 spare cards are set aside "gar nicht zum Spiele gerechnet", i.e. never used - so note 01 judges it not a rules ancestor) is [UNVERIFIED — review: asserted without a cite in this note] the same family, and the early Strohmandeln (dummy pile, winner takes top card) is closely parallel to the
   Piedmontese two-hand **Morto** of 1787 (undealt 25 cards as a stock; trick-winner takes the top card, shows it, adds it to his tricks) [06-S1].
   So a "dead man" did name a two-hand Tarock game - in Italian, in Piedmont (Morto) - and the German "Strohmann" is the same idea in
   straw-effigy dress. (The German word for the whist dummy is NOT required for this; see 5 below.)
6. **German "Strohmann" for the whist dummy is attested by 1839** (Bohemia, Prague: "spielte die Partie mit Strohmann zu Ende") and again in 1842, 1843,
   1853, 1857, 1858 [06-S4b]. Grimm's DWB has its own sense 5 "beim whistspiel", citing Raabe *Hungerpastor* (1864), Pückler (1873),
   Ebner-Eschenbach (1893) [06-S7]. Adelung (1801) and Campe (1810) know only the straw-effigy / weakling senses [06-S8] [review: the saved Adelung text is cut off after "scarecrow"; only Campe's full entry was verified].  So the whist sense
   probably entered German in the 1830s, as a rendering of French *le mort* / English *dummy*; the straw-effigy and "front man" senses are much older (16th-17th c.) [06-S7; 06-S9].
7. **Czech: the two-hand Tarok game was called "Štromandl", glossed "Taroky ve dvou"** in a 1932 Czech household-and-etiquette compendium
   (contents p. 495 lists "Štromandl ... 370"; text p. 370, copyright-restricted online) [corrected from "contents entry p. 369" per review/RE.md 1.1] [06-S10]. Czech 1844 translators gloss English *whist dummy* as
   "hastroš" - a footnote in the Czech Oliver Twist [06-S4c]. I found no Czech "slaměný panák" for the game or the dummy.
8. **ANNO (Vienna press) shows the game played for small stakes by 1850** (Der Wanderer, 30 Nov 1850) [06-S17], and a pun-laden word by the 1850s-60s.
9. **Cultural life, from Brno/Moravian press 1880-1906:** a "hausbackenes" game (1880), a café game with a Kibitz (1894), a punning
   joke about a husband used "als Strohmann zur Strohmandl-Partie" (1893), a social club called "Strohmandl" (1906), and an 1893 Tarock congress
   that drew up uniform rules "für Strohmandl, Tapper, Königsrufer" [06-S4a]. Banter material: see section 6.

## 1. The family: two-player games with dummy piles, tableaux or dead hands

### 1.1 Mechanics catalogue (pagat's own classification)
- pagat.com's page on two-player card games lists the methods of adapting a game for two: "Dummy hands" (extra hands, "face down, face up or
  partly face up"), "Trick and draw", and "Packets on the table": "Each player is dealt a hand of cards plus a number of packets of cards which
  are placed in a row on the table with the top card of each packet face up ... When playing a card, you may use either a card from your hand or the top
  card of any of your packets. Whenever you play a card from a packet, you turn the next card of the packet (if any) face up", and calls this
  "probably the most recently developed method and ... one of the most successful" [06-S5a].
  (Note for the build: Strohmandeln is a *Packets-on-the-table* game with an extra *pull-in* rule.)

### 1.3 What the sources disagree about (rules menu for Strohmandeln later form)
All from Dummett, who collates Ulmann (both books), Bermann, Werner, Unger, Beck, Löw, Babsch, Widder Lajos (Hungarian), and the Piatnik leaflet [06-S1] unless marked.
- **Deal annulled** if either player has no Tarock in his 15 cards (stated by Dummett as a general rule) [06-S1]. Werner (read directly) does not mention an annulment [06-S2].
- **Both pass:** rule A = hands thrown in, rule B = play proceeds "ohne daß einer der Spieler aufnimmt" (Werner gives the latter: stille Partie) [06-S2]; Löw and Piatnik leaflet recognise only B [06-S1].
- **Bidding:** only "Ich nehme auf" (Dummett); Werner: "Aufnehmen, d. h. ein Spiel riskieren" - the **declarer ("Aufnehmer") scores 3 if he wins and pays 4 if he loses; a quiet game pays 2** [06-S2]. Dummett's table: Ulmann/standard 2/3; Werner 3/4 and 2 for quiet game; Unger and Widder Lajos allow only 2 to the opponent when the declarer loses [06-S1].
- **Win threshold:** 36 points needed out of 70; 35 loses for the declarer and in a quiet game it is a draw with the following game doubled (Werner) [06-S2; 06-S1].
- **Kontra:** only Löw lets the dealer say Kontra to the opponent's "Ich nehme auf" [06-S1].
- **Who leads:** Vorhand (non-dealer) per Werner, Beck, Babsch, Piatnik; Löw says the declarer leads if there was a bid [06-S1; 06-S2].
- **Last card of a pile:** Beck and Babsch: taken into hand as soon as exposed even if not King/Tarock; Werner: the last card of a pile, "wenn es sich nicht zur Aufnahme in die Handkarte eignet" (i.e. if it is NOT a King/Tarock), "muß ... so lange offen liegen bleiben, bis es gelegentlich gespielt wird" [06-S2; 06-S1]. [Review: the earlier version of this note dropped the condition and so made Werner contradict Beck/Babsch. Werner's text, read directly (3724659.txt, p. 133), is silent on a last card that IS a King or Tarock; Dummett's statement that "Werner says, on the contrary, that the last card ... remains face up ... even if it is a King or a Tarock" is Dummett's reading, not demonstrable from Werner's wording.] Dummett: "if the players have good memories, it does not make any difference."
- **Bonuses:** Pagat ultimo 1 game point (Ulmann: some 2; Beck: some 2-3; Unger 5), Troule or four Kings 1 each when declared in hand, no Köpfe bonus (Widder Lajos alone), Valat 12 (Werner, Unger) [06-S1]. Werner: Pagat can never be announced ultimo (an opponent may reveal a Tarock last from his talon) [06-S2].
- **Dealer rotation:** alternates (Werner and most); Löw: winner of the round deals next [06-S1; 06-S2].
- **Cards in a pile when the pile is exposed**: Werner says each player may use *the exposed top cards of the piles for following suit*, and "aus dem Talon bedienen muß" if there is a card of the led suit among the top cards and none in hand [06-S2]; Dummett says the same ("may be compelled to play one of them in order to follow suit or to trump") [06-S1].
- Werner's strategy notes: the game "erfordert ein genaues Tarokzählen und eine große Aufmerksamkeit auf jede einzelne Farbenkarte"; "Die größte Rolle ... spielt das Impassiren"; one should not rush to finish the talon when Pagat-Ultimo is threatened, because an unexposed Tarock may be hidden there; the talon "kommt nur in den seltensten Fällen rasch zum völligen Verbrauch" [06-S2].

### 1.4 Strohmann in *other* Tarock games (not just two-hand)
- Dummett: three-handed **Neunzehner-Rufen**: "The dealer deals four hands: that facing him is a dummy or straw man (Strohmann)"; the dummy hand is exposed when the bidding is done; the games "died out after having a brief vogue lasting apparently from about 1890 to 1910" [06-S1].
- A Brno newspaper of 22 Oct 1887 describes the same: Neunzehnerrufen "das en quatre mit fliegendem, en trois mit einem ständigen Strohmann und auch en deux mit zwei Strohmännern gespielt wird. Diese in Mähren so beliebte Tarockart ... Das 'Strohmann-Tarock' (en deux) läßt vermöge seiner ganz eigenartigen Zusammenstellung eine Fülle von Spielmöglichkeiten und Feinheiten zu und ist hiedurch zum Lieblingsspiele aller denkenden Tarockfreunde geworden." [06-S4a, OCR]
  Translation: "Neunzehnerrufen, which is played four-handed with a 'flying' Strohmann [OCR: the dummy rotates?], three-handed with a permanent Strohmann, and two-handed with two Strohmänner. ... The 'Strohmann-Tarock' (two-handed), because of its quite peculiar make-up, allows a wealth of possibilities and subtleties and has thereby become the favourite game of all thinking Tarock friends."
  [Review: the two sentences quoted are not adjacent in the source (an intervening sentence says "Diese in Mähren so beliebte Tarockart kann als eine gute Schule ... bezeichnet werden"), and "Strohmann-Tarock (en deux)" may refer to Neunzehnerrufen with two Strohmänner rather than to the pile game of Werner; the identification with the later Strohmandeln is not established by this passage.] This tells us that by 1887 the German word "Strohmann" in Moravian Tarock meant a *dummy hand* (a whist-style sense), and "en deux" was the period term for two-handed play. It also shows that "fliegender Strohmann" (flying dummy) was period jargon - the same phrase appears in whist in 1858: "das Spiel mit dem fliegenden Strohmann und mit Wahl der Trumpffarbe" [06-S4b].
- Hungarian: Dummett says the later Strohmandeln was known in Hungary as "Strohmandli" and that Widder Lajos (Hungarian author) described it; "It does not seem ever to have been known in Germany, but only in Austria and Hungary" [06-S1]. I could not reach Arcanum (Cloudflare block) to see Widder Lajos or the 1888 Parlaghy Kálmán *Kártyajátékok könyve* directly.
- Tapp-Tarock (parent): named by Dummett as Tarok-Tappen "in its earliest recorded form" in the 1829 Neuestes allgemeines Spielbuch and the rest, but not in the 1821 Anweisung [06-S1]. The 1887 Brno article calls "Tapp-Tarock" the "Vater der jetzt landläufigen Tarock-Arten" which "nur den 'Dreier' und den 'Solo' kannte" and where the declarer was not allowed to look at the lower half of the talon [06-S4a, OCR].
- pagat on Tapp Tarock: "This three-handed Austrian Tarock game is not often played" - no rules there; rules are in an archived Krüpl page [06-S5i].

### 1.5 Danish / Swedish / Russian / Polish relatives
- **Sjavs / Tarok in Denmark**: two-player Sjavs has a four-pile face-up layout (table above). Danish Tarok is played with 78-card French Tarot cards, three players [06-S5d; 06-S5j]. I found no two-hand Danish *tarok* game.
- **Polish/Russian**: Tysiąc has two-player variants "Tysiąc licytowany" (two face-down 2-card talons) and "Tysiąc dobierany" (draw), plus Elwood's Russian two-hand version with two 3-card kitties - these are talon variants, not tableaux [06-S5k]. Czech Mariáš has a "lízaný mariáš" (draw) for two [06-S5l]. Hungarian "Felsős/Tartli" is a two-player trick-and-draw Jass [06-S5m].
- Pagat's French Tarot for two (Crossley) is the only 78-card two-hand tableau game in modern use that I found [06-S5b].
- Two-hand Handjass (Switzerland) and Gaigel/Binokel two-hand: no tableau (spare blind hand, stock) [06-S5f]. I did not find a two-hand Binokel or Doppelkopf with a tableau in pagat's pages.

## 2. Etymology and history of "Strohmann" / "Strohmandl" in German

### 2.1 Dictionaries
- **Grimm DWB** (Band 19, Sp. 1671, s.v. *strohmann*, m.; fetched through the Trier open API, reconstructed word by word from the kwic stream; the saved text is a token-merge and slightly rough) [06-S7]:
  - Opens "vir stramineus Stieler (1691) 1237. nl. strooman, fries. strieman, engl. strawman, dän. straamand."
  - Sense 1: "wie strohmeier" - an old office-holder ("ein mair, ein widemann, der strohmann", 1381).
  - Sense 2: "strohpuppe": (a) in mock fights and folk customs (Luther: "stromenner flechten wider die feinde"), as figure of mockery, as "schandzeichen" for disgraced girls (Chomel 1750), as phantom; (b) scarecrow ("vogelscheuche": Lehmann 1662, Schwerz 1825, with the diminutive "strohmännlein" Zendorius 1682).
  - Sense 3: "vorgeschobene person ... vgl. frz. homme de paille 'prêtenom' (1598) ... Furetière (1690)", used by Holtei, Strauß, Bismarck (1850) etc.
  - Sense 4: "unvermögender mensch" - weakling (Campe 1810; Suevus 1588; Dannhawer 1657; Gottsched 1740; the phrase "hölzerner, impotenter mann" is DWB's own gloss for a sub-sense, not Gottsched's words).
  - **Sense 5: "beim whistspiel die person, welche die partie des bloß fingierten partners (des 'blinden') übernimmt; (whist) kann auch unter drei teilnehmern mit strohmann gespielt werden"**, with Raabe, *Hungerpastor* (1864): "spielte mit dem ersten verwalter, dem pastor und dem strohmann whist"; Pückler *Briefwechsel* (1873): "wer mich ... mit der fürstin whist à deux spielen sähe, wo fräulein Lucie und Billy strohmänner dazu agiren"; Ebner-Eschenbach (1893): "zur whistpartie mit dem strohmann geladen". 
  - (No mention of Strohmandl or Tarok. DWB gives "blinden" as an alternative German whist term - cf. Danish/Norwegian "blind(emann)" below.)
- **Adelung** (Grammatisch-kritisches Wörterbuch, vol. 4, 1801 ed.): "Der Strohmann ... das Bild eines Mannes aus Stroh, so wie man es zu Verscheuchung der Vögel in die Felder und ..." - scarecrow only (saved text truncated by the API) [06-S8].
- **Campe** (Wörterbuch der deutschen Sprache, 1810, 4:718a): "das Bild eines Mannes aus Stroh, dergleichen man zur Verscheuchung der Vögel auf Feldern und in Gärten aufstellt. Uneigentlich, ein einfältiger, an Geist und Körper schwacher Mann." No card sense, no front-man sense [06-S8].
- **Pfeifer / DWDS**: "Strohmann m. 'Puppe aus Stroh' (16. Jh.), 'Vogelscheuche' (17. Jh.), 'vorgeschobene Person' (18. Jh.), wohl nach frz. homme de paille (Ende 16. Jh.)" [06-S9]. The DWDS entry does not mention cards.
- **Schmeller, Bayerisches Wörterbuch** vol. 3 (Stuttgart/Tübingen 1836): the lemma *Das Strö* (Stroh) with compounds (Strö-Kirchtag, Strö-Jungfer, Strö-Kranz, Strö-Truben ...); I found **no *Strohmann* entry** in my OCR of vol. 3 (OCR is poor, so this is a weak negative) [06-S11]. WBÖ (Wörterbuch der bairischen Mundarten in Österreich): I could not reach any online entry for *Strohmann*/*Strohmandl*; a web search surfaced none. [Gap]
- **Meyers Konversations-Lexikon**, vol. 20 p. 580 (the de.wikipedia article dates this 1908), s.v. *Whist*: "Neben dem W. unter vier Personen ist auch das W. mit Strohmann (Holzmann) unter dreien sehr gebräuchlich. Wer zuerst mit dem Strohmann zusammenspielt (König ist), gibt an." [06-S12] The de.wikipedia article (text derived from Meyers) says "Whist mit Strohmann, Mohr (Le mort) oder Blindem" [06-S12b, secondary].

[06-S4c]. "hastroš" is a Czech dialect word for a simpleton/straw-man figure - not verified here [UNVERIFIED sense].

### 2.3 What the evidence says about the direction of borrowing
- Pfeifer: "vorgeschobene Person" sense dated 18th c. "wohl nach frz. homme de paille" [06-S9]; DWB agrees, quoting Furetière 1690 [06-S7].
- For the whist sense: the German is a calque/analogue of French *le mort* (and English *dummy*): the 1858 text uses both "todten Mann" and "Strohmann" for the same role [06-S4b], the Meyers entry equates Strohmann/Holzmann [06-S12], and de.wikipedia glosses "(Le mort)" [06-S12b].
- For the *Tarock* use (Strohmandeln): the 1824 name appears long before the 1839 whist attestation I found - so the card-game Strohmann/Strohmandel need not derive from the whist term. It is likelier from the folk Strohmann (straw effigy/stand-in) used for the dead 25-card hand of the two-hand Tarok of c.1750-1800 (a stand-in third player). That is a hunch; no source states it. The diminutive -mandl/-mandeln is Bavarian-Austrian (as in "Schwammerl"); Werner confirms "in der Umgangssprache Strohmanndl" [06-S2].

## 3. Parallel words in other languages (as far as I could verify)

- **French**: *le mort* ("the dead [man]") for the bridge/whist dummy [06-S13]. The French Wiktionary gives it as the bridge sense of *mort*: "Joueur qui n'a pas à participer au jeu de la carte pendant le tour." French *homme de paille* is the "front man" parallel (Furetière 1690, per DWB) [06-S7; 06-S13]. A French Tarot "à deux" with a *mort* is described by de Gébelin (1781) as "a dead hand of twenty-five cards" [06-S1]. Gallica could not be searched (blocked).
- **English**: *dummy* (from *dumb* + -y per Wiktionary) with translations: French *mort*, Italian *morto*, Spanish *muerto*, Norwegian *blindemann*, Polish *dziadek*, Russian *болван* [06-S13b, UGC]. "Straw man" in English is the rhetorical sense; DWB lists *engl. strawman* as a cognate of the effigy [06-S7].
- **Italian**: *morto* is the bridge dummy [06-S13b, UGC]; and, directly relevant, **"Morto" is the name of a two-handed Piedmontese Tarocchi game (1787)** in which the 25 undealt cards form a face-down stock from which the trick-winner takes the top card - i.e. a two-hand Tarot named after a dead man [06-S1]. Dummett also notes "Two-handed Mitigati with the Morto" in the 1830 *Regole inalterabili* [06-S1].
- **Danish/Norwegian**: *blind* / *blinde* for the dummy (lex.dk: the declarer's partner "bliver 'blind', hvorefter spilfører styrer begge hænder"); Norwegian *blindemann* (snl.no) [06-S14]. - **Polish**: *dziadek* ("grandfather/old man") for the bridge dummy (pl.wikipedia, citing Pottage 2009) [06-S15]; Wiktionary translation table agrees [06-S13b]. No Polish game name found.

## 4. Hunches followed

- **"Is the dead-man dummy of Ombre/Quadrille-for-two or 'Taroc en deux' 1800 the root?"** - Partly yes, with a twist. Pagat's L'Hombre page mentions a "Mort" variant, but that is a four-player device (the fourth player takes the 13 talon cards and plays as declarer), not a dummy for two; the 1824 Haas L'Hombre book gives two-hand L'Hombre by removing a red suit, with no dummy [06-S5n; 06-S3]. The solid line is Tarot, not Ombre: de Gébelin's 1781 *mort*, the German *tote Hand* of 1800, the Piedmontese *Morto* (1787) - same idea: the third hand of a three-hand game is replaced by a dead hand. Dummett says explicitly that de Gébelin's game "has been rather mechanically adapted from a three-handed game" and that the German one is "so similar that they must have had a common origin" [06-S1]. I propose the early Strohmandeln (dummy pile, top card to the winner's tricks) is the Viennese descendant of that line, and that the later Strohmandeln replaced the dummy with six piles that the player digs through. (No source states the last link.)
- **Did another language name a two-hand Tarock after a straw/dead man?** Yes - Italian *Morto* (Piedmont, 1787) [06-S1]. French: de Gébelin uses "mort" for the hand, not as a game name [06-S1]. Hungarian: *Strohmandli* is a loan of the German name [06-S1]. Czech: *Štromandl* [06-S10].
- **Does "Strohmann" for whist predate "Strohmandeln"?** No - the reverse in print: Strohmandeln 1824 [06-S3] vs. whist-Strohmann 1839 [06-S4b].
- **Mährisch-Schlesischer Correspondent 1893 pun**: a woman candidate for the Reichsrat says "Glauben Sie ja nicht, daß ich Ihnen ihn [my husband] als Strohmann zur Strohmandl-Partie da lassen werde" - a double pun on Strohmann as husband/stand-in and as the game [06-S4a]. Nothing more to be had from this.
- **Moravian "Strohmandl" as a club name**: 1906 *Bohemia* mentions a "Geselligkeitsverein 'Strohmandl'" - a social club named after the game or the word [06-S4a]. Left there.
- **A "Reinhold Scheffel Strohmandl"** (Volkskalender story, 1884) is not a card game; ignored [06-S4a].
- **Zwei-hand Tarok and "Skis"**: not followed.

## 6. Banter material from this strand (period usage, kind only)

- "Ich nehme auf" - the only bid in the later game [06-S1; 06-S2]. Declarer "Aufnehmer", the quiet game "stille Partie", the piles "Talonhäufchen", the exposed card "Deckblatt" [06-S2].
- Period slang: "Impassiren" (the squeezing finesse; "Die größte Rolle beim Strohmann-Tarok spielt das Impassiren") [06-S2]; "Troule" and "die 4 Könige" as the Honneurs; "Pagat-Ultimo" [06-S2]. "Kibitz" (kiebitz) for an onlooker [06-S4a: "Unser Kibitz machte mir zwar den Antrag, er wolle alle Tage mit mir 'Strohmandel' spielen"].
- Tone: the game as "hausbacken" (homespun) (1880), a "Lieblingsspiel aller denkenden Tarockfreunde" (1887), "Strohmanndl" played by two commercial travellers on a travelling rug on a train, "so überwältigt, daß sie vergaßen, die Tarok zu zählen" (1928, WWI memoir) [06-S4a]. Fine for light banter about counting the Tarocks and the "Deckblatt".
- Kindness rule: the Dummett/Werner descriptions contain nothing unsuitable; the 1840s "Strohmann = filler partner at whist" usage is mildly demeaning of the person who is the Strohmann; I would not use it for the opponent.
