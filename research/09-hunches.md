## Best of (ranked by usability in the game)

1. BANTER / opponent catchphrases, all from the Mayr/Sedlaczek book on Tarock sayings via a review [09-S5]:
   - "Zieh endlich deine Fäustlinge aus!" ("Take your mittens off at last!") said to someone who keeps fumbling the deal. Speed ribbing, kind-rule safe.
   - "Wenn meine Großmutter vier Radln hätt', wär's a Autobus!" ("If my grandmother had four wheels she'd be a bus") for someone who analyses a lost hand too long. Perfect for the tutor/post-mortem screen.
   - "Spiel! I muss morgen um 8 Uhr im Büro sein" ("Play! I have to be at the office at 8 tomorrow") as the impatient-opponent timer nag.
   - "Zeig ihn her, auch wenn er noch so klein ist" ("Show it, however small it is") when you hesitate to play a low trump. [REVIEW RA: the review (09-S5) lists it only among the impatient "play already" nags ("oder grober"), with no meaning given; the "low trump" gloss is the note author's. "grober" (cruder) and the phrase has an obvious double entendre: do NOT use as a game line without a source for its meaning.]
   - "Mit voller Hos'n ist leicht stink'n" (earthy proverb) to deflate a gloating winner. Mildly crude; use at your discretion.
2. LOADING-SCREEN / tutor anecdote: Sigmund Freud's Saturday Tarock, with his worn cards and the retort "Lassen Sie uns doch das bisschen Behagen in der Unkultur!" (a pun on "Das Unbehagen in der Kultur") [REVIEW RA: the 2006 WZ article only says he "soll ... geantwortet haben" (is said to have replied) to a partner's criticism, with no source; the 2024 UNESCO report by the same authors words it differently ("Lassen Sie mir doch das bisschen Behagen in der Unkultur") and says his partners complained of the worn cards at the Berggasse. Anecdote, same two authors, not independent, not dated; treat as folklore]; even better, he pencilled the first-use date on every card-pack wrapper, and his pack is documented as Piatnik "Allerfeinste Klubtarock" with gilt edges [09-S2]. Also his letter to Ferenczi, [09-S2] [REVIEW RA: the Ferenczi letter is known only as a quotation in a newspaper article by Mayr & Sedlaczek, undated, no edition/page cited; the UNESCO report by the same authors says only "an einen ungarischen Kollegen". Not found in the Freud-Ferenczi Briefwechsel by RA (web search did not surface it); [UNVERIFIED] as a primary text].
3. STRAW-MAN WORD PLAY / visual motif: "Mandl" is Bavarian-Austrian for a little man-shaped thing "such as a Heumandl or a scarecrow" [09-S8]; "Strohmann" in the sense "straw doll" is attested from the 16th century, "scarecrow" from the 17th, "front man" from the 19th [09-S8]. So a scarecrow/haystack-figure motif for the three dummy packets is lexically apt. (Whether Strohmandl meant a stook of sheaves: not found, see Gaps.)
4. EASTER EGG, 1848 deck: Dr Anton Elfinger ("Cajetan"), Viennese physician and caricaturist, drew a Tarock pack with scenes of the 1848 Vienna Revolution; in a house search in 1850 it was found, confiscated and never returned [09-S11]. A "confiscated pack" achievement/lore card.
5. BANTER / catchphrase from the stage: "Ihr Blatt, Herr Rosenblatt!" ("Your deal/hand, Herr Rosenblatt!"), the ritual opening line of the card scene in Preses/Becher's play "Der Bockerer" (1948), repeated in the 1981 film as "Alsdann gehen mer. Ihr Blatt, Herr Rosenblatt." [09-S1]. Note "Blatt" = the hand dealt/to be played; a lovely opening line for a computer opponent.
6. ATMOSPHERE: Gartenlaube 1869: "das Fallen der Karten, das Rasseln der Sechser, die Seufzer der Verlierenden" ("the falling of the cards, the rattling of the six-kreuzer coins, the sighs of the losers"), snoring dog under the table, tobacco clouds, "a curious mixture of zeal and boredom" [09-S14]. Sound-design brief in one paragraph.
7. VISUAL: "Tarock mit Wiener Ausrufern", Vienna, c.1880, maker Josef Glanz, designed by Hans Schließmann: trumps show Viennese street types with their catch-cries ("Bitte sehr, Bitte gleich" for the waiter, "Straßen-Concert" for the organ-grinder, "Wächter der Sicherheit" for the policeman) [09-S24]. Source of period shouts for a character portrait set.
8. EASTER EGG, name: "Quapil" (Tarock IIII) is a German spelling of the Czech surname Kvapil, "he has hurried" [09-S28, S6] [REVIEW RA: 09-S28 is a family-name dictionary entry that says nothing about the card; the card-name link is the note author's inference, [UNVERIFIED]]. A character named Herr Quapil who "always hurries" writes itself (but nobody knows why the card got the name, [09-S6]).
9. EASTER EGG, Hegel: the young Hegel was known in Tübingen as "an eager, fine Tarok player" [09-S15]. And the Munich opera intendant Poißl kept to a "regelmäßigen Tarok" [09-S18].

## 1. Famous players and what is actually documented

- Freud. Wiener Zeitung 9 Mar 2006 (Mayr & Sedlaczek): Freud wrote to Sandor Ferenczi: "Samstag abends, nach elfstündiger Analysearbeit und am Ende einer Woche ohne Sonntag bin ich nicht zu gebrauchen und tue gut, Kartenspielen zu gehen" (Saturday evening, after eleven hours of analysis and the end of a week without a Sunday, I am no use and do well to go play cards). He often went to Vienna coffee houses, e.g. Café Landtmann; at home in the Berggasse, mostly Saturdays, coffee and biscuits were served, then a rich supper and wine. Biographers think him a passionate but not great player; played for honour, not money. Four wooden "Tarocktazzerln" (trays) survive, one scorched at the edge as an ashtray; a wooden games chest also held a small card press to stop the cards warping [09-S2]. The sources I could read say "Tarock/Koenigrufen"; the article's exact variant is stated as Koenigrufen [09-S2, S1]. The 2024 UNESCO expert report quotes the same letter [09-S1]. Mayr/Sedlaczek's table of contents lists a chapter "Wie man wilde Blumen erkennt, wie man Pilze findet und wie man Tarock spielt" (weekly Tarock round as Freud's relaxation) [09-S4, TOC].
- Others named in the Mayr/Sedlaczek book (contents/review only, no primary quotations seen): Mozart, Nestroy ("he almost always lost"), Johann Strauss (more serious at Tarock than at the harmonium), Radetzky (not as successful at Tarock as on the battlefield; lost half his fortune gambling per the chapter title), Karl Lueger, Marie von Ebner-Eschenbach ("Spielen! Spielen! Wir sind nicht da, um uns zu unterhalten"), Alfred Kubin, Torberg, Handke, Lore Krainer [09-S4 TOC, S5 review]. A chapter title also says Radetzky "Das halbe Vermögen verspielt" [REVIEW RA: TOC wording; 09 had "verspielte das halbe Vermögen"] [09-S4]. A chapter on Lueger quotes a dialect rebuke: "Häst z'erscht 's Anmäuerln g'lernt, bevorst a Kartn in d' Hand nimmst!" (You should have learned the hand-wall game first before taking a card in hand) [09-S4 TOC]; usable as a flavour line only after the exact source is read.
- Hegel as a student in Tuebingen: "eifrigen, feinen Tarokspieler" (Max Ring, Gartenlaube 1859) [09-S15]; the ADB Hegel entry adds that many who found him genial over a Schoppen "oder Tarok spielen sah" might have thought him capable of nothing else [09-S15].
- Mozart: the book lists Mozart's "Tarockscherze" with his sister Nannerl, quoting "hierkleid spielen und tarock gespieben" [09-S4 TOC]. A Mozarteum database search for "tarock" returned nothing (spelling variants likely) [UNVERIFIED] and the podcast claim about "Herecloth" in his diary could not be re-fetched (404). Treat as lead only.
- Beethoven, Metternich, Mahler, Schoenberg, Franz Joseph, Kafka, Musil: nothing found linking them to Tarock in any source read. Search engines turned up only Schoenberg designing his own (non-Tarock) cards c.1909 [UNVERIFIED, search snippet only]. Not usable.
- Chancellors etc. (Raab, Vranitzky, Schuessel, Gusenbauer), Schuessel designed his own pack for charity [09-S6]; modern, low priority.
- Sisi and Goethe appear as "Tarock players" in the book's blurb search result [UNVERIFIED].

## 2. The 'Rostopschin' bonus and the bird names

- Torberg, "Die Tante Jolesch" (1975), p. 217, as quoted in two Wikipedias: the master players of Café Central invented an "unbelievably complicated" Koenigrufen variant called "Rostopschin": besides Pagat ultimo and an "Uhu pre-ultimo", a higher bonus went to whoever took two tricks in a row in the course of the game with trumps XVII and XVIII, not announced in advance but with "Ross!" on the first card and "Topschin!" on the second, "sonst galt's nicht" [09-S7, S6] [2nd; Torberg's text itself not fetched (archive.org copy is borrow-only, 401)]. Sedlaczek says Torberg, a non-player, probably confused a bonus name with the whole game [09-S6, S7]. The German Wikipedia glossary says Rostopschin is "an old bonus: two tricks in a row with XVII and XVI" - divergent trump numbers (XVII+XVIII vs XVII+XVI) [09-S7]. Game idea: a joke "house bonus" for two consecutive trump tricks, announced by shouting "Ross!" then "Topschin!".
- Who was Rostopchin: Count Fyodor Rostopchin, governor-general of Moscow in 1812 when Napoleon came; the French attributed the burning of Moscow to his "ferocious patriotism"; in 1814 the family left Russia for Warsaw, the German Confederation, Vienna, Italy and finally Paris (1817) [09-S27]. So the name is the "man who burned Moscow", and the family sojourn in Vienna is a plausible route for the name; no source states why Tarock players used it [UNVERIFIED link].
- Birds: Pagat (I), Uhu (II), Kakadu/Pelikan/Kanari (III), Quapil/Marabu/Kikeriki/Wildsau (IIII) [09-S6, S31]. The Uhu name first appears in 1902 in the Hungarian game Alsos; Uhu on Tarock II is first printed 1937 (Unger); "bird" names for higher trumps were often chosen by syllable count; a 'Dodo' (Dronte) for V is also reported [09-S6]. The Pagat's nickname "Spatz" (sparrow) is what generated the "Voegel" idea [09-S6, S7]. The Tyrol Hopfgarten tournament calls Tarock III "Maus" (mouse) [09-S7]. Pagat.com's list prints "Marabu" for IIII [09-S31].
- Quapil = Czech surname Kvapil, "er ist geeilt" [09-S28] (the dictionary entry is a family-name entry, not about the card).

## 3. Table talk, idioms (UNESCO and book contents)

- UNESCO inventory text: "ein Vogerl zu machen", "den Gstieß zu schlagen", "den Pagat heimgehen zu lassen" (to send the sparrow home) [09-S3].
- Chapter titles of the Mayr/Sedlaczek book promise: "Bei Hof speisen", "Der König stinkt", "Ein Stich ist ein Walzer" as Tarockierer idioms; "Der Gstieß in der Alltagssprache" ("jemandem den Gstieß geben/reiben"); "Drei Mal mit dem Mond stechen is a Gemeinheit!" (cheating anecdote); "Tarock ha'm S' a kane? ..." (a train-ride Tarock tale); and the joke "Caveles?" - "Cafe Abeles!" [09-S4 TOC]. Meanings not given in the TOC; idea-seeds only.
- Review adds: cowardly players are "Staudenhocker" (bush-sitters); you can "pflanzen" (tease) opponents; table is mostly in the coffee house [09-S5 review]. "Staudenhocker" is about play style (kind-rule OK), but mild.
- Meyers 1889: a player who exchanges the Skis says "Ich skisiere (exkusiere) mich!" ("I excuse myself!") [09-S17]. Delightful line for the Sküs-exchange move, if the game has one.
- Haferl-Tarok (small-stakes pot game, in a Heyse novella of 1899) [09-S16]. Meaning: "Haferl" = little pot/mug; the context shows a casual game among travellers and the local teacher in an inn; exact stakes not stated.

## 4. Literature, theatre, caricature: atmosphere

- Schnitzler, "Lieutenant Gustl" (S. Fischer ed. 1906): at dawn the officer goes into "mein Kaffeehaus": "Da hinten ist der Tisch, wo die immer Tarok spielen"; later the waiter says the baker who died of a stroke is the stout man who "jeden Nachmittag neben die Herren Offiziere seine Tarokpartie hat ... mit'n Herrn Schlesinger und 'n Herrn Wasner von der Kunstblumenhandlung vis-a-vis" [09-S10]. Great officers'-mess/Kaffeehaus mood: names for NPC opponents (the baker, Schlesinger, Wasner).
- Vollmondzauber (Gartenlaube-type novel, c.1900), a colonel's cosy "Tarockpartie" interrupted by the post: "Der Oberst hatte soeben einen großartigen Pagat gemacht" [09-S10b: de.wikisource, text id "Vollmondzauber/Viertes Kapitel"; author not given in the extracted header, so treat attribution as UNVERIFIED]. Usable as a banter line: "Ein großartiger Pagat!".
- Heigel, "Der Schatten" (Gartenlaube 1864): two valets and the narrator play their evening Tarok in the anteroom, always ending in a quarrel; one valet then accuses the other of stealing the dead master's gold watch [09-S13]. Shows Tarok as the servants' game too (class-neutral).
- Gartenlaube 1869 "Regentage im Gebirge": a rainy afternoon in the mountains must have its "tarokt"; a mixed table of "Hochgeborene, Wohlgeborene und Eingeborene"; "es giebt ja auch Tarok-Amazonen" (there are also female Tarok players); the table silently "Drei- oder Viergespann" [09-S14]. Women playing is thus attested in 1869.
- Speidel (Ludwig Hevesi's obituary 1908): "Oft spielten wir Tarok. Wie er das erlernt hat, ist mir ein Rätsel. Er spielte es freilich, sagen wir, in seiner Weise" (we often played Tarok; how he learned it is a riddle; he played it in his own way) [09-S12]. Nice kind-rule-safe tutor remark: "He played it in his own way."
- BLKÖ on J. B. Moser (Viennese comic-writer): a booklet titled "Die Tarok- und Hundert-Eins-Brüder" ("The Tarok and Hundred-and-One brothers"; 'Hundert-Eins' is a card game) in his 16th volume, with "Die Kaffee- und Lotterieschwestern" [09-S19]. Date not given in the entry I read; lead for ANNO.
- Tapp Tarock: "Tarocktappen" in the caricature opera Perinet, "Die neue Alzeste" (Vienna 1806, p.77) and in Richter's "Briefe eines Eipeldauers" (1806, p.19); Meisl, "Amor und Psyche" (Pest 1820) has Jupiter refer to Tarocktappen; the oldest rules 1821 [09-S23, 2nd]. Tapp Tarock was the preferred game of the Viennese coffee houses before 1938 [09-S23]. Original texts not fetched.
- Herzmanovsky-Orlando invented a dream-country "Tarockei" ("Tarockia") whose structure is based on the card pack; Torberg renamed it "Tarockanien" (after Musil's Kakanien); the Wien Geschichte Wiki says Torberg coined Tarockanien and that nowhere else has Tarock so fed myth and cultural self-staging as in Austria [09-S29, S30]. The Mayr/Sedlaczek TOC adds that in his realm "vier Koenige aus dem Volke und der Sküs als Reichskanzler" rule [09-S4 TOC]. Visual/world-building motif: the Sküs as Chancellor.
- "Der Bockerer" (1948 play; TV 1963; film 1981): framework is a Dreiertarock round; the lawyer Dr Rosenblatt emigrates and returns as a US sergeant; "Ihr Blatt, Herr Rosenblatt" [09-S1]. Handke's "Der Chinese des Schmerzes": Tarock lets you "die Farben zeigen, sie zugeben und, vor allem, lakonisch sein" (show suits, follow suit and above all be laconic) [09-S1]. The Handke line is a compact piece of tutor wisdom about suit-following.
- Czech side: Karel Kramar as a schoolboy played taroky with his bench-mates during the lessons of the short-sighted catechist Drozd (classmate's memoir) [09-S20]; at the table of Havlicek Borovsky (biography by Tuma, chap. XXXVI) they were "Hrajice zvykle v taroky" (playing taroky as usual) joked unconcernedly at their table while spies and a drunken hero baited them nearby [09-S20]; Ott's Czech encyclopaedia (1902) defines "Pagat" as "ve hre tarokach karta oznacena I." [09-S20]. Meyrink's Czech-translated "Golem" (Prusik) uses a Tarok pack as a story prop: "Pagat? Co se mi zdalo bilou krabickou, byla tarokova hra", and a character asks "pane Cvaku, hrajete taroky?" - "To se vi. Od mladi." [09-S20 search snippets; only snippets read]. Czech in Austria-Hungary played taroky in classrooms, pubs and parlours.
- Hasek's Svejk: searched for taroky scenes, found nothing; sources describe mariáš and a cards-loss scene (Katz loses Svejk to Lukas) but no Tarock [UNVERIFIED; not read in the original]. Dead end.

## 5. Hungarian tarokk

- Legend: the name Paskievics derives from the Russian field marshal Paskevich who suppressed the 1848-49 revolution; another legend says the XX is the strongest "ally" one bitterly calls on (Russia). Hungarian Wikipedia says both are "only legends": the game was known years before 1848 and appears in print in 1844 and 1845 as "Paskievics-parti", "Paskievics-fele negyes tarokk" (Palffy Albert in Eletkepek 1844; Pesti Divatlap 18 Aug 1844 and 7 Aug 1845) [09-S21]. Pagat.com repeats the Paskevich origin and a speculation linking the 48 points needed to win to the year 1848 [09-S22]. Tutor fact with a twist.
- "Jokai-harmas" (Jokai three-hand tarokk), 42 cards: the most famous card company of the era (Nedeczky Istvan, Svab Karoly, Tisza Kalman, Jokai Mor; Mikszath: "Ez a leghiresebb parti a klubban...") was painted by Artur Ferraris as "A tortenelmi tarokkparti"; a politician was practically obliged to know it, and it was "shameful" not to be able to join a spontaneous tarokk game [09-S21]. Played for money settlements but "not for money" (gentry and intellectuals) [09-S21]. Arcanum anecdotes: not reachable (paywall); not followed.

## 6. Kiebitz etiquette, coffee-house custom

- At card games a Kiebitz (onlooker) is usually only allowed to look into one player's hand, to avoid signalling; the word is probably only folk-etymologically tied to the bird (Kluge: from Rotwelsch) [09-S26] [2nd]. A "1583 tavern notice" about Kiebitze sits in the German Wikipedia source as commented-out (unsourced) text: NOT to be used [09-S26].
- Torberg's Jolesch "Kaffeehaus" Kiebitz: not fetched (borrow-only archive.org). Saved search attempts returned errors.
- Bavarian Alps: "Wenn der Tarok nicht waere, dann wuerden die Berge noch viel einsamer sein" ("Without the Tarok the mountains would be lonelier still") [09-S14b: Gartenlaube-style "Bilder aus den deutschen Alpen", only a search snippet read; UNVERIFIED context].

## 7. Piquet cross-reference and the straw-man vocabulary

- The 1839 Neuestes Spielbuch sneer: "would probably only be chosen by people who are completely unaware of the noble game of Piquet" [09-S25, 2nd via en.wikipedia]; the same article gives "Skartindel" for low-count cards and says the 1829 game was easy to win if the straw-man pile yielded many kings [09-S25]. Cross-game joke for the series: the Strohmandeln opponent could mutter "Piquet-Kenner wuerden das nicht spielen" ("Piquet connoisseurs wouldn't play this"), or the Piquet game could have its opponent say it has "never heard of that Strohmandeln".
- Vocabulary check: DWDS lists "Strohmann" (1) front man, (2) "Puppe aus Stroh(ballen)" [09-S9]. Wiktionary (de): "Strohmann" = human figure made of straw; "Mandl" = Bavarian: small man, or a man-shaped thing "wie ein Heumandl oder eine Vogelscheuche", and male animal [09-S8]. German Wikipedia derives Strohmann from Roman Argei straw dolls thrown into the Tiber on the Ides of May and notes Stieler (1691) giving Latin vir stramineus [09-S8b: de.wikipedia "Strohmann"]. Harvest-custom link (stook, Garbenmandl) not found.

## 8. Card tax, sharping, music, caricature - what yielded nothing

- Card tax stamps: German Wikipedia on playing cards describes the tax (France from 1583; German Reich uniform 30/60 pfennig from 1 Jan 1879, abolished 1 Aug 1939) [09-S33]; the Austrian stamp (Klaus Reisinger, "Der oesterreichische Spielkarten-Steuerstempel", Wien 1990) was only a bibliographic hit [UNVERIFIED]. Note: the pack's "tax-stamped card" detail could be a hidden visual (an ace with an eagle stamp), but I could not verify the Austrian form. The one German-law Wikisource text found is a Reich law in Mark, not Austrian.
- Card-sharping court cases: only a literary hit, the Mayr/Sedlaczek anecdote chapter "Drei Mal mit dem Mond stechen is a Gemeinheit!" [09-S4 TOC]. No court record found.
- Tarock music: searches for polkas/couplets/Wienerlieder returned nothing; the book lists an Adolf Mueller jun. operetta with a closing aria built around "ein konfliktgeladenes Tarockspiel" and the cry "So spielt nur ein Krokodil!" (only a crocodile plays like that) [09-S4 TOC]; Strauss reportedly cared more for Tarock than composing ("Ich reiss dir das Beuschel aus dem Leib!") [09-S4 TOC]. Leads only.
- Caricature: the Simplicissimus index has a 1930s drawing "Beim Tarock" (F. Bleyer) and Jugend 1911-29 a piece "Tarock und Pflicht" (A. De Nora) [09-S34 index pages]; the Elfinger 1848 caricaturist deck is the best 19th c. hit [09-S11]. Images not examined.

- Freud: confirmed with primary-quoted letter text (via 2006 article) and a nice detail about the pack and pencilled dates. YIELD: high.
- Strohmandl = scarecrow/stook: Wiktionary/DWDS confirm the scarecrow and "Heumandl" senses; "stook of grain sheaves" for "Strohmandl" itself NOT confirmed. YIELD: medium (supports scarecrow motif only).
